## olympic-games.ts

- **Claim:** "The double-length race was added at the fourteenth Games, the long race at the fifteenth, and wrestling, boxing, the pentathlon, the chariot race and the brutal all-in fighting called the *pankration* followed over the next century and a half."
- **Problem:** Pausanias' own dates make the span shorter. Pentathlon and wrestling came at the 18th Olympiad (708 BC), boxing at the 23rd (688), the four-horse chariot at the 25th (680), and pankration at the 33rd (648). That is 128 years after 776, or about 75 years after the 14th Games, so "a century and a half" overstates it.
- **Evidence:** Pausanias 5.8.7–5.9.1 (corpus): "At the eighteenth Festival they remembered the pentathlum and wrestling… At the twenty-third… boxing… At the twenty-fifth… the race of full-grown horses… At the eighth Festival after this they admitted the pancratium".
- **Suggested fix:** "…followed over the next century or so (the pankration last, in 648 BC)."
- **Confidence:** medium

Checked, no problems found in: the Coroebus and stadion passage (Paus. 5.8.6); Hippias and Plutarch, Numa 1.4; the wild olive behind the temple's rear chamber (5.15.3); Herodotus 8.26 (Arcadian deserters, Tigranes' saying); Apology 36 (prytaneum meals, Olympic chariot victors); Thucydides 5.49 (420 BC, Lepreum, the truce, 2,000 minas, the Spartans excluded); the Zanes (5.21.2–4: fines, six statues in Ol. 98 = 388 BC, Eupolus and three boxers); the bases still in place by the stadium entrance; the Mount Typaeum law (5.6.7); maidens allowed and the priestess of Demeter Chamyne (6.20.9); Callipateira (5.6.8); Cynisca (3.8.1; victories in 396 and 392; her inscribed base); the {debated} labels on 776 and AD 393; the timeline (temple c. 470–456, excavations 1875–1881); and the bibliography (Miller 2004 Yale, Swaddling 1999 BM Press). Image panathenaic-runners matches Met 14.130.12 (Euphiletos Painter, c. 530 BC).

## mytilene.ts

- **Claim (hook):** "Its crew rowed without stopping for a day and a night, eating as they rowed."
- **Problem:** Thucydides gives no length for the second ship's voyage. "A day and a night" is the head start of the first ship (3.49.2), not the duration of the second ship's rowing. The hook merges the two.
- **Evidence:** Thuc. 3.49.2–3 (the first ship was ahead "by about a day and a night"; the second crew ate barley kneaded with wine and oil as they rowed, and slept and rowed in turns).
- **Suggested fix:** "It had a day and a night to make up; its crew rowed in shifts, eating as they rowed."
- **Confidence:** medium

- **Claim (timeline):** "Diodotus wins by a hair; the second ship catches the first."
- **Problem:** It did not catch it. The first ship arrived first, and the second put in just after, as Paches was about to act (3.49.4). The body text says this correctly.
- **Evidence:** Thuc. 3.49.4: "although the former did in fact arrive first… the second put in close after it".
- **Suggested fix:** "…the second ship arrives just in time."
- **Confidence:** medium

Checked, no problems found in: Mytilene's privileged status and the revolt of 428; the surrender to Paches; 3.36.2–4 (anger, men of military age, repentance next day); 3.36.3 (trireme sent "with all haste"); 3.36.6 (Cleon, most violent and most persuasive); Diodotus' arguments (3.44–48); the near-tie (3.49.1); the reward and barley (3.49.3); the executions of "somewhat more than a thousand" on Cleon's motion, the walls and the fleet (3.50.1); the land lots (3.50.2); the {debated} note on the numeral. ## asclepius.ts

- **Claim:** "The only first-hand description of what happened at night is a comedy."
- **Problem:** This is not true. Aelius Aristides' *Sacred Tales* (2nd century AD) give a long first-person account of his own nights of incubation and dreams at the Asclepieion of Pergamon. The Epidaurus cure inscriptions also describe the dreams, though not first-hand. The *Wealth* is the only detailed account from classical Athens, and it is fiction.
- **Evidence:** Aelius Aristides, *Hieroi Logoi* (Orations 47–52 Keil); see Edelstein and Edelstein 1945, which the entry already cites.
- **Suggested fix:** "The fullest description from classical Athens is a comedy." Optionally add that Aelius Aristides later wrote a first-hand account of his own nights in the god's hall at Pergamon.
- **Confidence:** high

- **Claim:** "doctors honoured Asclepius as their patron, and the Hippocratic Oath begins with his name."
- **Problem:** The Oath begins with Apollo. Asclepius is named second.
- **Evidence:** Hippocratic Oath (corpus, tlg0627.tlg013): "I SWEAR by Apollo the physician, and Aesculapius, and Health, and All-heal…"
- **Suggested fix:** "…and the Hippocratic Oath invokes him in its first line, after Apollo."
- **Confidence:** high

Checked, no problems found in: Iliad 4.194 (blameless physician; Machaon); Pindar, Pythian 3.55 (gold, raising the dead, the thunderbolt); Plutarch, Numa 4.6 (the Greek ἐπιξενωθῆναι supports "gave the god hospitality", although Perrin's translation obscures it); the cult in Athens 420 BC; Pausanias 2.27.1–3 (no births or deaths, the chryselephantine statue with staff and serpent by Thrasymedes, the sleeping-place, the six stelae; the stelae sentence is in 2.27.3); the Epidaurus cures (Cleo pregnant five years, the "Apistos" cure, excavated by Kavvadias from 1881); *Wealth* 653–747 (sea bath, beds of leaves, the porridge pot, the priest at 676–681, Panakeia, the two snakes at 733); Phaedo 118. Bibliography: Edelstein 1945, LiDonnici 1995. Image asklepios-epidaurus (NAM, Roman copy) is consistent.

## kleroterion.ts

- **Claim:** "The chief magistrates, the nine archons, were first chosen by lot, from men nominated by the villages, in [487/6 BC](cts:tlg0086.tlg003:22.5)." Timeline: "487/6 BC — The archons are first chosen by lot from nominated candidates."
- **Problem:** The cited source says this was the first time *since the tyranny* (τότε μετὰ τὴν τυραννίδα πρῶτον). The same work says Solon had already made the archons κληρωτὰς ἐκ προκρίτων (8.1). Some modern scholars doubt the Solonian lot, but the citation does not support "first" without qualification.
- **Evidence:** Ath. Pol. 22.5: "ἐκυάμευσαν τοὺς ἐννέα ἄρχοντας… τότε μετὰ τὴν τυραννίδα πρῶτον"; Ath. Pol. 8.1: "τὰς δʼ ἀρχὰς ἐποίησε κληρωτὰς ἐκ προκρίτων".
- **Suggested fix:** "…were chosen by lot, from men nominated by the demes, in 487/6 BC: according to the *Constitution of the Athenians*, the first time since the tyranny." Change the timeline to match.
- **Confidence:** high (on what the source says)

- **Claim:** "{well} Fragments of stone *kleroteria* were found in the American excavations of the Athenian Agora, which began in 1931, with rows of narrow slots exactly as the text describes…" Timeline: "4th c. BC — Stone allotment machines are in use for the courts."
- **Problem:** The surviving stone kleroteria are dated to the Hellenistic period (3rd–2nd century BC), after the *Constitution* was written. They vary in their number of columns: Dow catalogued fragments with one, two and four columns, and one with nearly 1,000 slots. The *Constitution* describes five columns (κανονίδες) per machine. They are evidence for the device, not an exact match for the 4th-century court procedure, and nothing proves that stone machines were used for the courts in the 4th century.
- **Evidence:** Wikipedia, "Kleroterion" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kleroterion): first found in the 1930s, "dating them to the second century BC"; Dow's 1937 catalogue of eleven fragments with differing column counts. The Greek Ministry of Culture describes Agora I 3967 as "Hellenistic period, 3rd century B.C." (seen in search results; the page itself would not load). Ath. Pol. 64.2: "εἰσὶ δὲ κανονίδες πέντε ἐν ἑκάστῳ τῶν κληρωτηρίων".
- **Suggested fix:** "Fragments of stone *kleroteria*, mostly of the third and second centuries BC, were found in the Agora excavations, with rows of narrow slots for the tickets; Sterling Dow's study of 1939 compared them with the *Constitution*'s account." Timeline: "4th c. BC — Allotment machines in use for the courts (described in the *Constitution*)", and "1930s — Hellenistic fragments of kleroteria found in the Agora."
- **Confidence:** medium

Checked, no problems found in: Herodotus 3.80.6 (Otanes: offices by lot, accountability, deliberation in common); Politics 4.1294b quotation; Ath. Pol. 24.3 (700 home officials); 62.3 (Council only twice; military offices repeatable); Xenophon, Mem. 1.2.9 (the accuser, choosing by bean); 63.4 (boxwood pinakion, name, father, deme, a letter up to Κ, ten sections); 64.2 (the *empēktēs* chosen by lot "so that the same man may not always do the inserting and cheat"); 64.3 (bronze cubes, black and white, one per five tickets); 64.4 (acorn tokens); 65.1 (staff the colour of the court); the generals elected; the "chosen by the bean" note. Bibliography: Dow, HSCP 50 (1939) 1–34; Hansen 1991; Rhodes 1981. The image (Agora Museum kleroterion) is correctly described.

**Count: 2 findings.**

## pythagoras.ts

- **Claim:** "he had been Euphorbus, a Trojan [wounded by Menelaus](cts:tlg0004.tlg001:8.1.4) in the *Iliad*."
- **Problem:** Diogenes Laertius (from Heraclides) does say "wounded", but in the *Iliad*, which the sentence names, Menelaus kills Euphorbus (Iliad 17.43–60). Readers will take "wounded" as a description of the Iliad scene.
- **Evidence:** DL 8.4: "his soul entered into Euphorbus and he was wounded by Menelaus"; Iliad 17, where Menelaus spears Euphorbus through the neck and strips his armour.
- **Suggested fix:** "…Euphorbus, the Trojan whom Menelaus kills in the *Iliad*."
- **Confidence:** medium

- **Claim:** "Above all, Diogenes Laertius says, Pythagoras [forbade beans](cts:tlg0004.tlg001:8.1.19)."
- **Problem:** In 8.19 the words "above all" (πάντων δὲ μάλιστα) govern red mullet and blacktail. Beans come afterwards in a list with hearts. The passage does not single beans out.
- **Evidence:** DL 8.19 (corpus): "Above all, he forbade as food red mullet and blacktail, and he enjoined abstinence from the hearts of animals and from beans".
- **Suggested fix:** "Diogenes Laertius says Pythagoras forbade red mullet, hearts and [beans](…)". The bean rule's later fame can be stated separately.
- **Confidence:** medium

- **Claim:** "The poet Xenophanes, a younger contemporary, mocked his belief…" and timeline "late 6th c. BC — Xenophanes mocks…" ({well}).
- **Problem:** The ancient dates make Xenophanes (born c. 570) Pythagoras' rough contemporary, not clearly younger. The date of the poem (fr. 7) is unknown: Xenophanes lived into the 470s, so "late 6th c." is a guess labelled as well established.
- **Evidence:** Standard chronology (Xenophanes c. 570–c. 475 BC; Pythagoras c. 570–c. 495 BC); no ancient date is given for the fragment.
- **Suggested fix:** "a contemporary"; timeline "late 6th or early 5th c. BC", or mark it debated.
- **Confidence:** low

- **Claim (timeline):** "3rd c. AD — Diogenes Laertius, Porphyry and Iamblichus write lives of Pythagoras."
- **Problem:** Iamblichus (c. 245–c. 325) probably wrote *On the Pythagorean Life* in the early 4th century.
- **Evidence:** Standard dating of Iamblichus.
- **Suggested fix:** "3rd–4th c. AD".
- **Confidence:** low

Checked, no problems found in: Heraclitus' "much learning" fragment (DL 9.1); the puppy fragment (DL 8.36); the rules in 8.17 (fire and knife, hearts) and the crumbs (8.34); Aristotle's list of reasons for the bean rule (8.34) and the hedged note on "oligarchic"; the death in the bean field (8.39); Dicaearchus and Heraclides on starvation at Metapontum, and Hermippus on the Agrigentine war (8.40); Apollodorus on the oxen (8.12); Babylonian knowledge of the rule; the suppression of the societies; "seven or eight centuries later". Bibliography: Burkert 1972 Harvard, Kahn 2001 Hackett.

**Count: 4 findings.**

- **Confidence:** medium (that the dial names more than four games); low (that leaving out Rhodes misleads)

Bibliography: Price 1974 (TAPS 64.7), Freeth 2006 (Nature 444, 587–591), Freeth 2008 (Nature 454, 614–617), Freeth 2021 (Sci. Rep. 11, 5821), Jones 2017 OUP. The image is consistent.

## trial-of-socrates.ts

- **Claim:** "{debated} … Others reply that the ancient sources hardly mention this, and that the charge should be taken at its word."
- **Problem:** Ancient sources do mention the political link explicitly. In 345 BC Aeschines tells an Athenian jury that they executed "Socrates the sophist" because he had taught Critias. Xenophon reports that the accuser blamed Socrates for Critias and Alcibiades. The real argument in the debate is that the link is absent from the indictment and from Plato's *Apology*, and is put forward by hostile or later speakers.
- **Evidence:** Aeschines 1.173 (corpus): "Did you put to death Socrates the sophist… because he was shown to have been the teacher of Critias, one of the Thirty…"; Xenophon, Mem. 1.2.12: "Among the associates of Socrates were Critias and Alcibiades…" (the accuser's charge).
- **Suggested fix:** "Others reply that the indictment and Plato's *Apology* say nothing of politics (the link appears in the accuser quoted by Xenophon and, fifty years later, in a speech by Aeschines), and that the charge should be taken at its word."
- **Confidence:** high (that the sources mention it); the wording of the fix is a suggestion

- **Claim:** "Then, at his friends' urging, he offered a fine of [thirty minas]…"
- **Problem:** This skips a step that matters for the "would not beg" theme. In Plato, Socrates first proposes a fine of one mina, all he could pay, and only then, at the urging of Plato, Crito, Critobulus and Apollodorus (Hicks: "Aristobulus" is a misprint), raises it to thirty.
- **Evidence:** Apology 38b (corpus): "I might perhaps pay a mina of silver. So I propose that penalty; but Plato here… tell me to propose a fine of thirty minas".
- **Suggested fix:** "Then he offered a fine of one mina, all he had, and at his friends' urging raised it to thirty, which Plato and others would guarantee."
- **Confidence:** low (an omission, not an error)

Checked, no problems found in: the indictment (DL 2.40, via Favorinus, in the Metroon); the three accusers and the groups they spoke for (Apology 23e–24a, within the linked section 23); the old slanders and *Clouds* (Apology 19c); the Delphic mission; the "unexamined life" (38a); "thirty votes" (36a); prytaneum meals (36d); thirty days and the Delian embassy (Xen. Mem. 4.8.2); Phaedo 115–118 and the last words; *to pharmakon* and the hemlock debate; the Thirty, Critias and Alcibiades, the amnesty of 403; the timeline. Bibliography: Brickhouse and Smith 1989 Princeton, Wilson 2007 Harvard.

**Count: 2 findings.**

## laurion.ts

- **Claim:** "{debated} … Both agree that the new ships were built for a war with the neighbouring island of Aegina, and that they fought at Salamis in 480 BC."
- **Problem:** The *Constitution of the Athenians* does not mention Aegina. In its version Themistocles lent a talent each to the hundred richest Athenians, each built one trireme, and "with these they fought at Salamis against the barbarians". Only Herodotus names the war with Aegina. The sources also disagree on more than the number of ships: one says a share-out of ten drachmas a man was stopped, the other describes loans to the hundred richest citizens.
- **Evidence:** Ath. Pol. 22.7 (corpus Greek): "…δανεῖσαι κελεύων τοῖς πλουσιωτάτοις Ἀθηναίων ἑκατὸν ἑκάστῳ τάλαντον… ἐναυπηγήσατο τριήρεις ἑκατόν… αἷς ἐναυμάχησαν ἐν Σαλαμῖνι πρὸς τοὺς βαρβάρους"; Herodotus 7.144.1–2: "for the war with Aegina".
- **Suggested fix:** "The two accounts disagree: the *Constitution* has a hundred ships, built by the hundred richest Athenians with a talent each; Herodotus has two hundred, built for the war with the neighbouring island of Aegina. Both say the ships fought at Salamis in 480 BC."
- **Confidence:** high

- **Claim:** "{well} … The owners, the contractors and the men who profited are named in the sources; the miners themselves are not."
- **Problem:** Some miners are named. The best known is the 4th-century BC gravestone of Atotas, a Paphlagonian who calls himself μεταλλεύς, "miner", found in the Laurion district. Other slave names appear on dedications from the mining area. "Rarely" is accurate; "not" is too strong for a {well} paragraph.
- **Evidence:** IG II² 10051 (epitaph of Atotas, "Ἀτώτας μεταλλεύς"), discussed in https://tristanklemmerhusby.weebly.com/blog-greco-roman-manumission/post-81-atotas-the-miner-and-slave-ethnicity-in-4th-century-athens and in Lauffer, *Die Bergwerkssklaven von Laureion*, which the entry cites.
- **Suggested fix:** "…the miners themselves are almost never named. One exception is a gravestone for Atotas, a Paphlagonian who proudly called himself a 'miner'."
- **Confidence:** medium-high

Checked, no problems found in: Xenophon, Poroi 4.2 (no one dates the start of mining); Maroneia and the 100-talent surplus (Ath. Pol. 22.7, archon Nicodemus = 483/2); Herodotus 7.144.1 (ten drachmas each, two hundred ships); Nicias' thousand men let to Sosias the Thracian at an obol a day, numbers kept up (4.14); Hipponicus 600 and Philemonides 300 (4.15); three public slaves per citizen (4.17); *Poroi* c. 355 BC; Decelea in 413 BC; Thuc. 7.27.5 (more than 20,000 deserted, "a large proportion" craftsmen); the washeries at Thorikos. Bibliography: Lauffer 1979, Conophagos 1980, Rhodes 1981. Image athenian-owl matches Met 2021.40.80 (tetradrachm, c. 440–404 BC).
